Senet

Region or tradition: Ancient Egypt

Objective in our teaching version: Get all seven pieces through the last houses and off the board.

Ancient Egyptian boards preserve thirty houses and a variety of equipment and marks. Senet later acquired associations with the journey into the afterlife. This table follows the Guild’s simplified Kendall-based teaching draft, via Damian Walker. The full ancient rules are unknown.

Royal Game of Ur

Region or tradition: Ancient Mesopotamia

Objective in our teaching version: Bring all seven counters off the far end.

Boards and counters were excavated from the Royal Cemetery at Ur in present-day Iraq. A much later Babylonian tablet preserves rules for a related game. This seven-counter, four-tetrahedron table uses the popular Finkel-style short-route reconstruction.

Hounds and Jackals

Region or tradition: Egypt and the Near East

Objective in our teaching version: Guide five hound or jackal pegs to the shared goal.

The Met’s Theban set preserves a 58-hole board with carved hound and jackal pegs. Similar boards are known across Egypt and the Near East. The board here is a playable diagram of that family. Four sticks, one-way shortcuts and the bonus holes belong to this reconstruction.

Ludus Duodecim Scriptorum

Region or tradition: Roman world

Objective in our teaching version: Bring all fifteen counters into the final six points, then bear them off.

Roman boards often have three rows of twelve marks or letters. The path and mechanics here are a modern reconstruction based on the published Austin/Murray route and common fifteen-counter rules. Choices about combining dice and priority re-entry are stated for this table.

Nine Men's Morris

Region or tradition: Medieval Europe and other traditions

Objective in our teaching version: Form mills and reduce the opponent to fewer than three pieces or block all legal moves after placement.

The game is documented in medieval European sources, including Alfonso X’s Libro de los juegos of 1283. Similar carved diagrams are difficult to date on their own. The flying rule and protected-piece conventions vary by ruleset.

Play and read the full rules

Pachisi

Region or tradition: Indian subcontinent

Objective in our teaching version: Bring all eight of your partnership’s pawns back to the charkoni.

Pachisi is a South Asian family of cloth-cross partnership games with many local variants. This table follows the six-cowrie Masters teaching version, with the documented last-square digital exception. The screen uses equal independent mouth and back outcomes for the shells.

Chaupar

Region or tradition: Indian subcontinent

Objective in our teaching version: Bring both partners’ pawns home. Combined pawns can take stronger groups.

Cross-shaped boards and long dice are documented in South Asia, including the Indian Board Game Survey. Chaupar practices vary by place. This table uses the Masters grouping, casting and partner-finish rules.

Yut Nori

Region or tradition: Korea

Objective in our teaching version: Bring all four mal around the board and home.

Yut Nori is a living Korean game played around Seollal and beyond. The National Folk Museum of Korea documents four sticks, four mal per side and a board of 29 dots. Shortcut and bonus conventions vary across tables.

Slahal / Lahal

Region or tradition: Pacific Northwest · Coast Salish communities

Objective in our teaching version: Win all ten tally sticks by choosing hidden hands and reading the other team.

Slahal is a living family of games with traditions that vary among Indigenous nations, communities and families. The Guild’s beginner draft teaches concealment and guessing with simplified scoring. Community gatherings can include drums, team songs, language, and distinct protocols. See the community sources for those traditions.

Astragaloi / Knucklebones

Region or tradition: Ancient Mediterranean

Objective in our teaching version: Be the first to cast all four different faces.

Sheep or goat ankle bones and shaped imitations survive from the ancient Mediterranean. They supported many games of chance and dexterity. The named Venus throw is documented in Roman accounts. This table models a casting activity.

How to read historical game evidence

A surviving board may reveal its shape, materials and layout without preserving the complete instructions for play. Written accounts, inscriptions and comparable traditions can help researchers develop teaching reconstructions. Living traditions, including Slahal and Yut Nori, should be understood in their community context rather than treated as a single fixed ancient rulebook.

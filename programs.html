Historical games, taught by playing

At the Cat v. Canary Guild, visitors don't need to know the rules before sitting down. Our volunteer guides introduce each game, explain its cultural and historical context, and help newcomers begin playing.

Our in-person teaching collection and online Game House overlap but are not identical. The ten online tables include games from Egypt, Mesopotamia, Rome, medieval Europe, India, Korea and the Pacific Northwest, with instructions and notes on historical evidence.

Play in the Traveling Game House

Games from around the world

Our teaching collection includes Senet from ancient Egypt, the Royal Game of Ur from Mesopotamia, Roman Duodecim Scripta, Chinese Xiangqi, the Maya-associated Puluc, Hnefatafl and Brandubh, Nine Men's Morris, Alquerque, and Liar's Dice.

For games whose original rules are not fully preserved, we explain the difference between historical evidence and modern reconstruction. We encourage curiosity rather than pretending every question has a certain answer.

Living-history demonstrations

We use handmade and reproduced game boards, pieces, and a furnished traveling pavilion to help visitors imagine how games brought people together. Lessons are interactive, informal, and free to participants.

Community and accessibility

The pavilion offers a place to sit, cool down, and connect. We welcome visitors who need a quieter moment during a busy faire, as well as performers and vendors taking a break.

Where to find us

Our public appearances are announced on the Guild's event section. Event admission and schedules are managed by the organizers of each faire; participation in Guild demonstrations is free.

At the table: a closer look

Senet: An ancient Egyptian game whose full original rules are not preserved; we identify modern reconstructions. Royal Game of Ur: A Mesopotamian race game with archaeological evidence for its history. Duodecim Scripta: A Roman game whose exact rules are still studied. Xiangqi: A living Chinese strategy tradition featuring generals, chariots, horses and cannons.

Hnefatafl and Brandubh: Asymmetric games of escape and capture, with historically varied forms. Nine Men's Morris and Alquerque: Placement and capture games. Puluc and Liar's Dice: Traditions inviting discussion of chance, strategy, and cultural context.

Free learning activities: history through play

Senet: archaeology and uncertainty

Look at a thirty-house Egyptian board and compare the marks on its squares. Before playing, ask which details can be observed in the surviving object and which must be inferred. Our teaching version uses a modern reconstruction because no complete ancient rules survive.

Try our Senet table · Explore the Met's historical context

Royal Game of Ur: chance and strategy

Players race seven counters along a shared route. A landing on a rosette can earn another throw; the central rosette offers protection in the version we teach. Compare a cautious move with a risky capture, and discuss how later written evidence helps reconstruct a much older game.

Try the Royal Game of Ur · See a surviving board at the British Museum

Nine Men's Morris: patterns and planning

Start with nine counters each. Take turns placing pieces, forming straight lines of three called mills, and removing an opposing piece after making a mill. When all pieces are placed, move along the board's lines. Discuss why blocking an opponent may be more useful than completing your own mill immediately.

Questions for a group lesson

  • What can a surviving board tell us about its players? What can it not tell us?
  • How do chance, strategy and cooperation differ across games?
  • What materials would players have had available in different places and periods?

These activities are free to use. No purchase, account or donation is required to access the online teaching tables.

How a demonstration works

Choose a game, sit with a volunteer guide, learn the goal, and begin playing. Some visitors stay for a quick round; others ask about archaeological evidence and historical uncertainty. We distinguish documented rules from modern interpretations rather than claiming certainty where none exists.

For educators and event organizers

Games can open discussions about everyday life, cross-cultural exchange, mathematics, and material culture. For a demonstration or educational partnership, contact the Foundation. Availability is confirmed individually.
